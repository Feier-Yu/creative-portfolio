Internal Colonialism and the Power of Culture

In Chekhov's Sakhalin Island, Chekhov takes on the role of an observer looking in on the Sakhalin region and the local people. His documentation of the native population reminded me a lot of the Japanese Edo-period children's story "Momotarō," where the Japanese boy Momotarō decides to battle an island of demons and ends up ruling over the land. You might be curious why I drew this connection. It's because "Momotarō" was rewritten as a justification for Japanese colonization in the 20th century. My ancestors were the demons of the story, succumbing to the power of a small Japanese boy because of how uncivilized they were.

To me, the level of dehumanization imposed on the Nivkh people in Chekhov's observations is no less demeaning than those imposed on my ancestors. The attributes which the author casually applies to the Nivkh people resemble observations of animals rather than ethnography. Particularly when he says, "His facial expression is not savage; it is always intelligent, gentle, naïvely attentive." The words "not savage" introduce a comparative scale, by which Chekhov is judging the people. It simultaneously implies that the Nivkh people were considered "savages" and that some people on Sakhalin Island are still "savages." Meanwhile, the phrase "naïvely attentive" infantilizes the Nivkh people and reduces them to a people in need of colonization. What does Chekhov's sentence even mean? Is Chekhov suggesting that the Gilyak people are better than the "savages" he expected them to be? Or is he complimenting these people to bear a likeness of the Russian expression but still unavoidably naïve? I do not know. What I do know is what I felt when first reading this line: the author believes that the Gilyak people, regardless of their efforts, will never amount to civilized cultural standards.

Because Alexander Etkind mentions Foucault's repressive hypothesis in his book Internal Colonization, I feel that it is appropriate to bring it up here. Culture represses politics but in turn helps its prominence grow. Chekhov is definitely considered one of Russia's most prominent authors and is deeply seated in Russian culture, but his documentations of the Nivkh people will also be of that same prominence. Vladimir Sangi is Nivkh. Do we consider his stories as "naïve" as Chekhov describes his people? However, as a writer, Sangi's works are unlikely to be on a level playing field with Chekhov's. Sangi's works can challenge the validity of Chekhov and repress colonial ideology yet never reach the same effectiveness Sakhalin Island has in upholding it. Chekhov's work has been translated into multiple languages and widely studied, while most people may never encounter Sangi's work unless they are in a very specific circumstance. When the colonial framework is the only framework that is given, how can we repress colonialism through language and achieve the result of decolonization? Culture is erased through colonialism, and a new culture is formed afterward. Some traditions manage to slip through the cracks, but it will never be as it was before. I'm not saying that this is necessarily a bad thing, though, as a cynic, I do not enjoy this kind of coerced process.

In the section "Syako" of the movie Seven Songs from the Tundra, Syako goes to see her friend Maima. This Nenets girl writes her new Russian name "Lida" in the Cyrillic alphabet on the snow, telling Syako to call her Lida from now on. Words hold meaning. Names hold meaning. The physical writing in snow will be submerged or melted someday. However, the name Lida will forever be a part of the Nenets culture now. It is unjust to erase that name as well. As the Nenets poet Yuri Vaella has written in his poem "On Things Eternal," "only the wind-beaten cradle will remind you of the child's cry, of lullabies sung by wise gray-haired women, and the unrealized life of a lost people." Notice how the word "sung" is in the past tense; time is of the essence here because women cannot sing their lullabies eternally. People die; memories fade. What is left afterwards can be altered over the course of decades. Is any culture truly eternal?

We can no longer remain observers as if the cultures that are jeopardized are not parts of our own. Neither preservation nor development seems to be the solution, as both are still framed under colonial ideologies. Because once you question what preservation and development embody, you soon realize they are both actions done to recover irrevocable damage. So, I guess subjective documentation is all that we can do to record culture as it is colonized, decolonized, and whatever follows afterward. What is important is that all these documentations are equally viewed and distributed, although the chance of this happening is highly unlikely. If you are what you speak, then what are all of us?
